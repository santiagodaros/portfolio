A backup that runs every night and finishes green says nothing about whether that organization can actually resume operating after a real incident. That's the gap this article walks through: the distance between "we have backups" and "we have continuity."

<PullQuote>
  A successful backup is a snapshot. Continuity is the full film: how long it takes you to get back on your feet, and how much you lost along the way.
</PullQuote>

## RPO and RTO are not the same thing

**RPO (Recovery Point Objective)** answers one question: how much data am I willing to lose? If the last valid backup is 4 hours old, that's your real RPO — not whatever the policy document says.

**RTO (Recovery Time Objective)** answers another: how long can the system stay down before restoring stops being a viable option? <sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>

The most common mistake in governance audits is treating these two numbers as one, or worse: never measuring either and assuming "we have backups" covers both.

<Callout variant="warning" lang="en">
  A daily backup with 30-day retention doesn't automatically define a 24-hour RPO. It only defines a 24-hour RPO **if the restore actually works** and **if someone has tested it**.
</Callout>

## The gap that shows up in production

In practice, three failures repeat across organizations that believe they've solved continuity:

1. The backup runs, but no one has ever restored it in a real environment.
2. The documented RTO doesn't account for decision time — escalation, approval, communication — only the technical restore time.
3. The critical dependency (a database, a Key Vault, an internal DNS) isn't covered by the same recovery plan as the application that depends on it.

| Scenario | Documented RPO | Real RPO (measured) | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Critical VM with daily backup | 24 h | 24 h | None |
| Database with log shipping | 15 min | 6 h (restore never tested) | 5 h 45 min |
| Storage account with weekly snapshot | 24 h | 7 days | 6 days |

<Takeaway>
  The RPO and RTO that matter are the ones **measured in a drill**, not the ones written into the backup policy the day it was drafted.
</Takeaway>

## How the gap gets closed

Closing this gap isn't a tooling problem — Azure Backup, AWS Backup, or Veeam solve the technical piece without much effort. The problem is governance: who owns the RTO for each system, and who tests it.

```
$ az backup job list --resource-group rg-prod --vault-name vault-prod --output table
Status      Operation     Item             Duration
Completed   Backup        vm-sql-prod01    00:42:11
Completed   Backup        vm-app-prod02    00:38:05
Failed      Restore-Test  vm-sql-prod01    —
```

That last `Failed` on a restore test is the line no written policy will ever show you — and it's the only one that matters when the incident is real. <sup><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup>

Real resilience is built on three simple practices, sustained over time:

- Scheduled restore drills, not just scheduled backups.
- RTO defined per critical system, not per generic infrastructure tier.
- A business owner — not just IT — who signs off on the tolerable RPO for each critical dependency. <sup><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup>

## Conclusion

Backup is not continuity. It's the raw material continuity is made of, and it only becomes continuity once someone tests, measures, and documents the real recovery time — not the one sitting in a policy PDF no one has opened since it was signed off. <sup><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup>
